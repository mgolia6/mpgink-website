WHERE IT CAME FROM

It started with a homework assignment.

Years before the first edition, Matthew sat around a long wooden table in Arnold Hall at Hartwick College, in a class taught by Professor C.W. “Sandy” Huntington. The assignment never changed, and it was never easy:

“His homework was simple in theory; the practice of completing it was altogether something else. Each week, he gave us seemingly disparate reading assignments and asked us to find the connections between them and write a paper about them.”

“When I started this newsletter in 2018 as a way to connect with a new team, I channeled that same energy — pulling together random facts, national days, births, and deaths, current events and searching for the common thread. Because of that, at least once a week, Sandy is with me — in my heart and in my mind.”

— EDITION #290, “BIG CHILL VIBES”

Sandy is gone now. The homework isn’t. Every edition since has been the same exercise: take a handful of unrelated things that happened to share a date, and go looking for the thread that ties them together. On the 290th edition Matthew guessed what his old professor would have made of it — “an encouraging B — recognizing the essence of something special, but nudging me toward refinement.”

HOW THE FORMAT MOVED
AUG 29, 2018 · EDITION 1
A daily, not a Friday

It went out every weekday to one team. Each one carried the national days, this-day-in-history, who was born, who died, a quote, and a riddle whose answer didn’t arrive until tomorrow’s edition. The first riddle answer was coffin. All 60 are in Wing C.

FALL 2018 · EDITIONS 1–60
Versioned like software

The index that tracked those first editions labels them Beta, then 1.0, 2.0, 3.0. The layout was being rewritten in public, one send at a time — each founding-era page prints the version it shipped under.

2021–2024 · THE EMAIL ERA
Daily becomes Friday

The cadence settled onto Fridays and the writing got longer — music, obituaries, history, and a personal story doing the connecting. 96 of these survive, recovered from the sent-mail archive.

JUN 28, 2024 · EDITION 252
Out of the inbox, into the open

The Twilight Zone Edition was the first to publish as a LinkedIn newsletter — the same homework, finally in public, and the point at which the visual card became part of the edition.

HOW IT'S MADE

Anatomy of an edition.

Four things have to be true before an edition goes out. The date has to actually hold something worth pulling on. The word has to be one you’d want to keep. The rebus has to be solvable but not obvious. And somewhere in it there has to be a real story, or the rest is just trivia with good typography.

01 — THE DATE
Born & remembered

Who entered and left the world on this date, and what their lives still have to say. This is the oldest part of the format — it ran in edition one and it has run in nearly every edition since.

The best editions happen when the date does the work on its own. April 4th holds both Maya Angelou's birth and Dr. King's assassination. March 12th holds Kerouac's birth, Charlie Parker's death, and the first step of the Salt March.

02 — THE WORD
One worth keeping

A word that earns its place in your vocabulary rather than just passing through it — kuleana, mondegreen, lackadaisical, ephemeral, nascent, tchotchke.

Sometimes it's the whole thesis. The Maui edition was built around kuleana — a Hawaiian word for one's personal sense of responsibility and sacred duty — because no English word was going to carry it.

03 — THE REBUS
The puzzle

A visual riddle to chew on. The answer is never given away easy — that's the point.

It's the one element that predates everything else. In 2018 it was a word riddle, and the answer didn't arrive until the next day's edition — a small reason to open tomorrow's. It became a rebus once the card did.

04 — THE ANCHOR
The personal story

The emotional core — a real story from a real life that makes the whole edition land. Without it the other three are trivia.

It's also the part that costs something: a professor's memorial, a rescue dog, a layoff with a 60-day clock on it, a son's first book. The anchor is why the thread holds.
