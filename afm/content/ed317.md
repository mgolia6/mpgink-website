# AFM | Safe at Home | #317

Happy Aloha Friday. 🌺

But first, the shoutout: this one’s for the 317 — Indianapolis, Indiana.

I was eight years old the summer I found out a kid from Connecticut could beat the world.

August 1989. Trumbull is a town down the road from Hamden, where I grew up, and that year [Trumbull National](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Little_League_World_Series) went to Williamsport and beat Kaohsiung, Taiwan — a program that had taken the previous three championships and did not lose games like that one. Final, 5–2. Nobody outside Fairfield County thought it was coming.

I’ve told this story before and I’ll tell it again, because every year when mid-August rolls around I’m driven back, like a line drive, to my youth. I put the games on. For a couple of hours at a stretch I’m eight again, which is the actual product the [Little League World Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_League_World_Series) sells and nobody advertises.

Here’s what I notice now that I didn’t notice then. It isn’t only the ferocity — though it’s there, worn on the sleeve, the kind of wanting that adulthood slowly teaches you to keep indoors. It’s what happens right after. A kid strikes out to end a season he waited his whole short life for, and he cries, and then he lines up and shakes twelve hands. The sportsmanship in that tournament is unmatched anywhere in sport, and it is performed almost entirely by twelve-year-olds.

[Ray Bradbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dandelion_Wine) was born this week in 1920. He spent a whole novel trying to bottle a single Illinois summer he was twelve years old for, and then spent the rest of his life insisting he’d never actually left it. That’s the standard nostalgia sets for itself: get back.

[Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thus_Spoke_Zarathustra) died this week in 1900, and he ruined that framing for me in the best way. He laid out three stages of the spirit — the camel, who carries what he’s given; the lion, who refuses it; and the child, who plays. What gets me is the order. The child is *last*. Not the thing you start as and lose. The thing you spend the whole climb trying to get back to, and only a few ever do.

Which means the kids in South Williamsport aren’t the beginning of the story. They’re the end of it. They’re what the camel and the lion are working toward.

So when I tune in Sunday I don’t think I’m chasing my own childhood, exactly. I think I’m watching twenty kids do the hardest thing on Nietzsche’s list, casually, in cleats, on basic cable — and then go be gracious about it. Then the last out is recorded, the dogpile happens, and that’s another ***summer in the books***.

Sunday afternoon, twenty kids in South Williamsport will finish something they’ll talk about for fifty years. Put the game on. Let whoever’s in the house drift in and out of the room. Then go outside while the light still holds, because there’s one more summer in the books and nobody gets to know it was a good one until later.

Mahalo Nui. 🌺

*— Matthew*
